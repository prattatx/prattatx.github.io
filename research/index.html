Measurement, and whether you can believe it

Three research lines, all about the same thing: does this instrument actually measure what it claims to measure? Sleep and shiftwork at UConn, visual cognition through the dissertation, then applied usability measurement at consumer scale. Twenty-six years later that question is still the one I ask first, whether the instrument is a scale, a survey, or a model.

M.A. thesis

UConn, 2000 · public, full text

Assessing Sleep Quality in Commercial Maritime Crewmembers: Is the Sleep Disturbance Scale a Valid Measure?

A psychometric validation study, not a sleep study. Earlier work had assembled four self-report items into a provisional Sleep Disturbance Scale and found no relationship between it and sleep length in maritime crews. That null had two readings: maritime duty does not hurt sleep quality, or the scale does not measure sleep quality. This thesis exists to decide which.

The method was a validity evidence chain. Convergent validity against personality and sleep-habit measures. Discriminant validity against things the scale should not track. Criterion validity against actual sleep length. Then a structural equation model fit against an established theoretical model.

Verdict: the scale is valid in this sample, so the original null is real. Maritime duty shortened sleep without degrading sleep quality. Four of ten predictions failed outright and the write-up says so plainly.

The mediation test used Baron and Kenny (1986). David Kenny sat on the committee and signed the thesis.

Three habits from this document are still in everything I do. Reliability is treated as necessary and nowhere near sufficient: the scale had a good alpha in two samples and I still spent thirty-three pages arguing that this proves nothing about validity. Response bias in high-stakes settings is named directly, because people whose livelihood depends on being fit will not self-report impairment. And the future-research section argues that every correlation in the study might be an artifact of everything being a self-report Likert item, and calls for a multitrait-multimethod matrix. That is the argument for simulation-based measurement over questionnaires, made twenty-six years early.

Doctoral dissertation

UConn, 2002

Development of Cognitive Styles and Strategies in the Visual Discrimination of Computer Displayed Graphics: Task Characteristics, Training and Design

Ph.D. in Industrial/Organizational Psychology, concentrating in human factors, judgment and decision making, data visualization, and UX research and design. The dissertation asks how people with different cognitive styles learn to read serially presented graphics, and what that means for how you design and train against a display.

Four of the nine publications below are the published spine of this work, run with R. James Holzworth and Young Woo Sohn.
