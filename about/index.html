About / Austin, Texas

A psychologist who builds AI products

I trained as an I/O psychologist at UConn: human factors, judgment and decision making, and measurement. Since then I have applied that training inside companies, most of which did not call it psychology.

I am Director, AI and Data Science at Charles Schwab, where I started in November 2026.

Before that I co-founded Fyve Health and was its CEO from 2025 to 2026. We built Tessa, a memory and caregiving companion for families with aging parents and for care communities. It worked by sound and processed audio on the device.

I spent eighteen years at AT&T, most of them in the Chief Data Office as Director of AI User Experience. The job had two halves. On the product side I led the team designing generative AI applications, data discovery engines, privacy systems and visualization for knowledge workers, plus eighteen platform capabilities for the company's AI-as-a-Service stack. On the device side I ran a usability benchmark program covering forty-plus phones a year, which identified $110M of annual cost avoidance, and the Iconic Device UX Program, which handled launches such as the original iPhone and the Kindle.

Much of that work was getting engineers and senior leaders who each held one piece of a problem to agree on a single account of it, and then measuring whether the result worked. The Data Powered white paper series, the AI Transformation Center of Excellence governance, the persona program and the analytics translator program all ran that way.

I also invented there. I am a named inventor on 358 granted U.S. patents, 226 of them still in force, and I was a top-10 inventor company-wide.

Before AT&T I was at Sony Online Entertainment, designing for massively multiplayer games. A redesign of the player experience there drove a 38% lift in subscription services. Before that I was at Lockheed Martin, leading UX research and design on the Navy's next-generation destroyer program: cognitive task analysis and human performance modeling, with a TS/SCI clearance. And before that I worked in the IBM usability lab while I was in the UConn master's program.

During COVID I was a partner in wisely.io, which built two AI products: a trust-scoring system that detected deepfakes and scored content trustworthiness, and a wearable for communication between children on the autism spectrum and their parents. Both were built and user-tested. Our NSF SBIR applications did not land, and the venture wound down on capital constraints.

In Austin I co-created AT&T Community Labs in 2014, an open-innovation program that paired the company's technical leaders with founders in the local startup community.

What ties it together

Most of the people I have studied were in places they could not easily leave: maritime watchkeepers on a ninety-day tour, Navy destroyer crews, and aging adults in their own homes. Sleep, fatigue, mood and the honesty of self-report run through all of it. Each time the question was whether the measurement could be believed.

My thesis ended by asking whether these measures could prevent an accident like the Exxon Valdez. The answer was no, not without measuring the effect on actual performance.

The research. The work. The patents.
