Venkatesh Rao's essay "Cognitive Mercantilism" (Contraptions, 25 October 2026) argues that much AI panic repeats a 17th-century mistake, which was to treat wealth as a fixed stock. Read it here four ways: plain text, a diagram, an interactive chart and a short narrated video. Each one carries the same argument, so start with whichever suits you.

The payoff is one question you can put to any claim about AI and work: what does it assume about demand?

Figure 1. One assumption, two futures. Both fears sit on the left branch. Rao's bet is the loop on the right.

The essay in plain text

Much AI panic treats intelligence the way 17th-century statesmen treated gold. Here is Rao's case in short sentences, then a test you can run on any claim about AI and work.

Venkatesh Rao uses a history of capitalism to explain one kind of AI panic. Before capitalism, mercantilists thought that wealth was a fixed stock of land and gold. So one nation could gain only when another nation lost.

Rao gives the name cognitive mercantilist to a person who thinks the same way about intelligence. This person thinks that intelligence is a fixed stock in a small number of brains. The goal is to hoard it, control the supply, and keep the price high. The person also thinks that most people cannot think well. So cheap AI will leave most minds with nothing to do.

History did not go that way for food. Farm work fell from most of the population to a small minority. The other people did not become useless. They found new work, and they got more food than before.

Rao asks a question about demand: how much intelligence can people find uses for? He thinks that this demand grows when the cost falls.

Test a claim about AI and work

  1. Find the claim about AI and work.
  2. Write down what the claim assumes about the demand for intelligence.
  3. If the claim needs a fixed demand, mark it as mercantilist.
  4. List the new things that people could want if intelligence costs less.
  5. Compare that list with the assumption before you accept the claim.

The core idea, from loose to strict plain English

The industrious loop

Rao's best evidence is a loop from Joyce Appleby's history of 18th-century Britain. New goods made people want more, so they chose to work more, which raised incomes and demand. Rao thinks cheap AI can start the same loop with thinking in place of farm days.

Tea, sugar and bright Indian cottons gave ordinary Britons new things to want. To buy them, many people chose to work more days, from about 250 a year to 300 in Appleby's account. More work raised their incomes. Higher incomes raised the demand for goods. That demand paid for more productive work, and the loop ran again.

This is a textbook constant-elasticity model added for this post, since Rao gives no elasticity number. At 0, uses stay fixed and work for minds falls in step with the price. Anywhere below 1, work for minds still shrinks as the price falls, which is the fear on the left of Figure 1. Above 1, it grows, which is Rao's bet. Worked by hand: at a price of 10% and an elasticity of 1.5, uses rise to 0.1−1.5 = 31.6 times today, and work for minds is 100 × 0.1−0.5 = 316.

The test to keep

Before you accept a story about AI and jobs, ask what it assumes about demand. If the story only works when the uses for intelligence stay fixed, it is the mercantilist story again, and farming is the counterexample: the share of people needed on farms collapsed, and people ended up with more food and new kinds of work.

Rao grants that freedom carries real risks and that powerful systems need limits. His claim is narrower. Risk alone does not prove that intelligence must stay scarce.
