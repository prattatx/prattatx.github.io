A quick roundup of research and news that caught my attention this week. The through-line: noninvasive signals are getting more credible as early-warning tools, large-scale real-world datasets are reshaping how we study dementia, and biomarkers are moving closer to symptoms rather than just pathology.

---

## 1. Brain age from sleep EEG may signal dementia risk

[News-Medical, 2026-03-19](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260319/Brain-age-from-sleep-patterns-may-signal-future-dementia-risk.aspx)

Researchers applied machine learning to sleep EEG recordings to estimate "brain age." When estimated brain age exceeded chronological age, future dementia risk went up — roughly **40% higher risk per 10-year gap**. The pooled dataset covered ~7,000 participants across five studies (ages 40–94, follow-up ranging from 3.5 to 17 years), with ~1,000 incident dementia cases.

The model used 13 microstructural EEG features rather than broad sleep metrics like sleep stages or efficiency. As Yue Leng put it: *"Broad sleep metrics don't fully capture the complex multidimensional nature of sleep physiology."*

The implication is that fine-grained passive signals — potentially from wearables — could flag risk in nonclinical settings long before symptoms appear.

## 2. Turning living rooms into dementia research labs

[HealthTechDigital, 2026-03-19](https://www.healthtechdigital.com/from-living-room-to-laboratory-new-dementia-study-to-close-participation-gap/)

A UK initiative (Cera, GlobalMinds/AkriviaHealth, Cardiff University) is trying to fix a persistent research blind spot: older adults account for two-thirds of illness in the UK but only one-third of clinical trial participants. Their approach is to bring the study into people's homes — capturing data during the "silent hours" outside clinics — and build a multimodal longitudinal dataset from NHS records, questionnaires, and biological samples including DNA.

Target: ~1,000 people living with dementia. Dr Ben Maruthappu's framing is memorable: *"We aren't just monitoring health; we are turning every living room into a laboratory for a cure."*

The partnership model here — home care provider + data platform + academic center — is worth watching as a template.

## 3. A metaplatform for Alzheimer's at 10-million-patient scale

[News-Medical, 2026-03-18](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260318/New-data-platform-transforms-prediction-and-understanding-of-Alzheimere28099s-disease.aspx)

Columbia and collaborators describe the M3AD Study: an effort to harmonize EHRs from nearly **10 million patients** across health systems in NYC, Chicago, and Miami — including ~60,000 with AD/ADRD. The platform treats Alzheimer's not as an isolated condition but as one node in a web of interacting chronic diseases, behaviors, and social circumstances.

A few things stand out: the scale (roughly 35% of Americans over 85 have Alzheimer's; more than 7.2 million older Americans are living with it today), the multimorbidity framing (~90% of adults over 60 have 2+ chronic diseases), and tools like **eRADAR** that flag potentially undiagnosed dementia from routine EHR data. The platform is designed to be extensible — imaging, genetics, and additional biomarkers can be layered in.

## 4. Siemens launches blood-based biomarker assays for brain health

[News-Medical, 2026-03-19](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260319/Siemens-Healthineers-launches-brain-health-research-portfolio-with-first-biomarker-assays-now-available.aspx)

Siemens Healthineers announced automated immunoassays for **phosphorylated tau 217 (pTau217)** and **brain-derived tau (BDTau)** running on their Atellica analyzer platform. The pitch is simpler scaling: blood tests instead of lumbar punctures for CSF.

Henrik Zetterberg's quote captures the appeal neatly: *"Blood tests are much easier for both patients and doctors — you can scale testing, follow patients, or perhaps prepare a biomarker portfolio."*

The entry of a major diagnostics incumbent into "brain health portfolios" as a product category signals that this market is maturing fast.

## 5. Synaptic CSF markers predict Alzheimer's progression with AUC ~0.8

[Alzforum, 2026-03-19](https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/can-synaptic-markers-forecast-worsening-alzheimers-disease)

A Nature Communications study evaluated CSF synaptic proteins **NPTX1** and **NPTXR** as progression markers across two cohorts (635 participants total, from China and Norway). Both proteins declined as AD worsened in amyloid-positive individuals, correlated with cortical thinning, and were associated with faster clinical decline.

Among MCI participants followed ~2.5 years, lower baseline levels predicted conversion to dementia with **AUC ≈ 0.8** — outperforming several classical CSF markers in that cohort.

Why this matters conceptually: synaptic damage is "closely tied to symptoms," so synaptic markers may be more proximal to functional decline than amyloid or tau alone. For staging and progression forecasting, that's a meaningful distinction.

## 6. Type 1 diabetes and dementia risk: nearly 3x

[News-Medical, 2026-03-18](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260318/People-with-type-1-diabetes-face-higher-risk-of-developing-dementia.aspx)

A large AAN study (~283,772 people, average age 64) found that people with type 1 diabetes were nearly **three times** as likely to develop dementia as those without diabetes — and the association appears stronger than for type 2 (roughly 2x). Over ~2.4 years average follow-up, incidence was 2.6% (T1D), 1.8% (T2D), 0.6% (no diabetes).

The usual caveats apply: this is association, not causation, and diagnoses came from EHR and survey data. But as a signal for risk stratification in older adults with metabolic comorbidities, it's notable.
